# How to negotiate a brand deal as a small YouTuber

Work out your fair price from your views before you reply, try to get the brand to share its budget first, then counter with a number backed by data. Most of the money in a deal isn't the base fee anyway: exclusivity, usage rights and payment terms are where small creators lose the most.

Here's a simple process you can follow on your next sponsor email.

## Step 1: Know your number before you reply

You can't negotiate if you don't know what you're worth. Work out three numbers:

- **Your target:** what a fair deal looks like.
- **Your opening counter:** a bit above target.
- **Your floor:** the lowest cash fee you'll take. Decide this before any call.

To get your target, use **(median views per video / 1,000) x CPM**. Use the median of your last 10 videos at about 30 days old, not your best video.

For the CPM, [ThoughtLeaders' data on 9,002 sold integrations](https://thoughtleaders.ai/blog/2026-10-15-youtube-sponsorship-cost-2026/) found a median of **$36 per 1,000 projected views** for channels under 100K subscribers, with a median deal of **$600** and the middle half between **$370 and $1,000**. Your niche shifts this up or down; [TrendReport's estimates](https://trendreport.com/intelligence/youtube-sponsorship-rates-by-niche/) run from $14-$38 for gaming to $18-$47 for business and finance.

## Step 2: Do some homework on the brand

[TubeBuddy's negotiation guide](https://www.tubebuddy.com/blog/how-to-negotiate-youtube-brand-deals-a-guide-for-creators/) suggests checking three things before you talk money:

- **Who else they've sponsored.** Search YouTube for the brand name plus "sponsored." That shows the formats and channel sizes they usually buy.
- **Your own numbers.** Median views, engagement and who your viewers are (age, country).
- **Their goal.** Are they after awareness or sales? Sales-focused brands (apps, software) often care about clicks and signups more than raw views, which changes what you should offer.

## Step 3: Let the brand name a number first

When a brand asks "what are your rates?", TubeBuddy suggests replying with something like: "I'd love to hear what budget you're working with so I can build the right package for you."

If they push, ask about their campaign goal and whether they have a range in mind. If they won't share, give your number, but give it as a range tied to formats. [Sponsorable's podcast guide](https://sponsorable.com/guides/podcast-ad-rates) makes the same point for podcasters: naming a range starts a negotiation, while a single number starts a discount.

Once a brand shares a figure, TubeBuddy's advice is to counter **20-30% higher** if it's already in the right range, and say why (your views, your audience, how past sponsors did).

## Step 4: Negotiate the terms, not just the price

This is where small creators give away the most value. The base fee should cover one placement in one video, posted on your channel. Everything else is extra.

**Exclusivity.** If the brand wants you to avoid competitors for a while, that costs you future deals. [1of10](https://www.1of10.com/blog/youtube-sponsorship-rates/) puts typical exclusivity at +20 to +50 percent; TubeBuddy says 30 days can add up to 50% and 90 days can effectively double your rate. Always get "competitor" defined in writing.

**Usage rights.** If the brand wants to run your segment as a paid ad, that's a separate license. 1of10 lists +30 to +100 percent; TubeBuddy suggests 30-50% more, with an end date. TubeBuddy says twelve months is pretty standard. "In perpetuity" means forever, and shouldn't be free.

**Revisions.** Two rounds is the common standard, per both TubeBuddy and [Influencer Advisory](https://influenceradvisory.com/blog/creator-rate-card-template/). Write down what counts as a revision. A brand-new creative direction after you've filmed isn't one.

**Deliverables and timeline.** How many mentions, how long, where in the video, and when it goes live. TubeBuddy suggests a publish window of about 30 days after script approval.

**Extras.** A pinned comment, description link or community post are add-ons. 1of10 suggests +5 to +10 percent for a description link plus pinned comment.

## Step 5: Handling a lowball offer

You have three options, per TubeBuddy:

1. **Counter with reasons.** Come back higher and tie it to results: your views, your audience, or how a past sponsor did. If it's your first deal, point to comparable rates instead; our [rates pages](/sponsorrateradar/rates/) link every number to its source.
2. **Cut the scope, not your rate.** If their budget is fixed, offer something smaller for their number, like a shorter mention instead of a full segment. Your per-unit rate stays the same.
3. **Walk away politely.** "This one isn't the right fit on budget, but I'd love to work together when timing aligns." Brands that won't move and want one-sided terms are often hard partners anyway.

**Free product offers.** These are common for small channels. 1of10 is blunt that free product alone is not payment. If you take one, treat it as a gift, not as your rate.

## Step 6: Protect your payment

The contract is where deals go wrong. Things to ask for:

- **Money up front.** Influencer Advisory gives 50% on signing and 50% on publish as a standard payment term. Sponsorable calls half up front and half on air "a reasonable and normal ask" for a first deal with an unknown brand.
- **A kill fee.** If the brand cancels after you've written or filmed, you should still be paid. TubeBuddy says kill fees are typically 25-50% of the deal. Influencer Advisory suggests 50% if cancelled after brief approval and 100% after a draft is delivered.
- **Approval on edits.** TubeBuddy flags "right to modify" clauses that let a brand edit your content or add voiceover. Ask for your written approval on any changes.
- **Careful with performance-only pay.** Payment tied only to clicks or promo codes puts the risk on you. If a brand insists, TubeBuddy suggests charging a higher base rate to cover it.

## Step 7: Make the second deal better

The first deal is mostly about doing a good job. The second is where you have leverage. ThoughtLeaders found that 35% of brands that booked YouTube sponsorships booked the same channel again 60 to 365 days later. If a brand comes back, the first deal worked for them, which is a good reason to raise your price.

## A quick script you can use

> Thanks for reaching out! I'd love to work together. Before I send rates, could you share your budget range and what you're hoping the video achieves (signups, sales, awareness)? For context, my last 10 videos have a median of [X] views at 30 days, mostly from [countries]. I usually do a 60-90 second integrated segment, and I'm happy to put together a package around your goals.

## FAQ

### Should I tell a brand my rate first?

Try not to. Ask for their budget and goal first. If they insist, give a range by format rather than one number.

### How much should I counter a brand deal offer?

If their offer is in the right range, TubeBuddy suggests countering 20-30% higher, with a reason tied to your views or audience.

### How much extra should I charge for exclusivity?

1of10 lists +20 to +50 percent as typical. TubeBuddy notes 90 days of exclusivity can effectively double your rate.

### Should I ask for payment up front?

Yes, especially with a new brand. 50% on signing and 50% on publish is a common and reasonable term.

### What's a fair rate for a small channel?

ThoughtLeaders' median for channels under 100K subscribers was $600 per integration. Run your own views through the [calculator](/sponsorrateradar/calculator.html) to get a number for your channel.
